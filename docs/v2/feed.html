The Kitchen Feed

A social layer built on the one thing KinniJije knows that no other food app does: what is in your kitchen before you cook. Not a plate feed — those lose to Instagram. A feed where the constraint is the content, and where the reply is more fun than the scroll.

Proposal — not approved Reuses rundown + decide history + uploads Blocked on public identity Needs variant bucketing first Email-only delivery · no push exists
01

Why most feeds fail, and why this one might not

Before the design, the honest risk. A social feed is the most-attempted and least-successful bolt-on in consumer apps, and the reason is always the same: a feed is only as good as its supply. The core loop here produces about one decision per user per day. At 10,000 users posting 0.3 times a day that is 3,000 items spread across 10,000 people with no follow graph — so most people open the feed, see nothing from anyone they care about, and never come back. No audience, no supply. No supply, no audience.

So the question is not "how do we add a feed". It is what does KinniJije already produce that is worth seeing? And there is a genuinely good answer, which is the throwaway line in the brief: people complimenting each other's ingredients.

The content unit: the constraint, not the plate

Every other food app's social layer is the finished dish, which makes it a beauty contest against Instagram — a contest we lose. Ours is the only app that knows the before. Nobody wants to post three tomatoes and half an onion to Instagram. But "look what I got out of this" is a real social moment: relatable, low-stakes, funny, and — critically — a question other people can answer.

The thesis in one line: a perfect jollof is boring. A decent jollof from four sad ingredients is a flex — and it is useful to the reader, because they have those same four ingredients tonight.
02

What the codebase already gives us

This matters more than the design, because it is the difference between a quarter and a fortnight. Four of the five hard parts are already built — and the fifth is a blocker, not a task.

Already built

A daily content stream

notifications/rundown.service.ts already generates a personalised morning message per user: weather, what is expiring, and three meal slots each with a written reason. insights.jobs.ts does a weekly summary on top.

This is the single most important finding. We do not have a cold-start problem the way most feeds do — we have a daily per-user stream with no social layer and nowhere to live. The cheap move is to give the rundown a home and let other people into it, not to build a second empty surface beside it.

Already built

Uploads, spines, delivery

  • Uploads: presign → confirm → derive, direct to S3/R2; bytes never touch the API. Reusable almost as-is for a cook photo (needs a new cook_photo purpose and a public-read policy — reads today are owner-scoped signed URLs).
  • AVATAR is a declared but unused FILE_PURPOSES value. Avatars today are generated blobatar blobs, not uploads.
  • The best spine is decide_history, not the cook log: it stores the inputs (kitchen, mood, weight, minutes) alongside the verdict, which is exactly the "before" a post needs.
  • Delivery: a DB-backed job queue with self-requeueing sweeps, plus a mature email policy with per-kind frequency caps and unsubscribe handling.
  • Analytics: ~90 named events mirrored client and server, Mixpanel with super-properties and identify.
Do not log feed analytics to decide_logs. That collection is deliberately anonymous — salted truncated IP hashes, no owner column — and its header says adding one would retract a privacy promise. Use decide_history or a new table.

The four things that are genuinely missing

Blocker · privacy

There is no public identity

UserView exposes email, name, prefs, city. There is no handle, no avatar, no bio, and no public/private flag. name is whatever somebody typed at signup — with zero expectation it would ever be shown to a stranger.

Publishing it next to their kitchen contents would publish data they never consented to publish. This needs an explicit opt-in and a separate handle — never a reuse of name.

The sharper problem: kitchen contents are economic data. "Half an onion and some garri" reads as this person cannot afford food. The sad-fridge framing is funny when you choose it and cruel when the system does it to you. See §05 — this shapes the whole design, it is not a footnote.
Blocker · measurement

Flags cannot run a real A/B test

lib/flags holds global booleans. There is no percentage rollout, no per-user bucketing, no variant assignment. DECIDE_INVITE is described in the code as "an experiment", but it can only be measured as everyone-or-nobody, before versus after.

That is a weak test: confounded by seasonality, by launch novelty, and by whatever else shipped that week. Every experiment in §06 needs variant assignment hashed on userId, with the variant stamped onto every Mixpanel event.

Build this first. Roughly 2–3 days in lib/flags plus the analytics boundary, and it pays for itself across every future experiment rather than just this one. Guests also need variant persistence, or the pre-signup funnel is unmeasurable.
Constraint · delivery

There is no push. At all.

There is a PWA manifest but no service worker, no FCM, no APNs, and no mobile app — web only. There is also no in-app inbox table, no unread count, no bell. The entire notification surface today is email, via Resend.

This directly weakens the highest-leverage part of the loop (§03 step 03). Email can carry a once-daily digest perfectly well, and the existing per-kind frequency caps make that safe — but it cannot do "ada.k just answered you" in the minutes that make a rescue feel alive.

Consequence for the design: rescues must work without real-time delivery. That is why KinniJije answers every rescue immediately (§04) — the asker gets something useful on the spot rather than waiting on a human and an email. Web push is a separate project; do not make the feed depend on it.
Constraint · nav and data

The tab bar is full, and the cook log is a stub

Five mobile tabs already: Decide · Kitchen · Saved · Market · You. A sixth is not an option, so a Kitchens tab means displacing one — which is a product argument, not a layout tweak, and a real cost I would not hide in an estimate. This is the strongest reason to run test 4's cheap arm first.

And CookedMealModel holds only ownerId, mealId, mealName, cookedAt. No photo, no note, no rating, no visibility. It does not record what was in the kitchen at the time, so the "before" has to be snapshotted at Cook this or it is lost.

A rating component already exists in the design system, unwired. Cheap to adopt, and it gives a post something to say when there is no photo.

One house rule this design must not break

image-pipeline.html states it plainly: never mix illustration and photography on one surface. Meal images are AI-generated and operator-reviewed; until a dish has photography the product uses hand-drawn illustrations. A timeline that interleaves real user photos with illustrated placeholders violates that rule as written.

How this design complies: the before/after cells are illustration-only until a cook attaches a photo, and when they do, the photo fills both cells' visual register — a post is either an illustrated pair or a photographed pair, never one of each. The ingredient chips carry the information either way, which is what lets a photo-less post still work.

03

The loop — where stickiness actually comes from

Feeds do not stick because of the feed. They stick because of the thing that pulls you back. The notification leg is not a polish item bolted on at the end — without it this is a bulletin board people check once and forget.

01
You post your kitchen
One tap from the decide flow. No photo needed — the data is already there.
02
Someone answers
A dish, or a reaction on one ingredient. KinniJije answers too, so it is never empty.
03
You get told
The load-bearing step — and the weakest. Email only, batched daily. No push exists, so the pot answering instantly is what covers the gap.
04
You come back and cook it
Straight into the existing decide verdict and recipe. No new surface.
05
You post the save
The before is still on file, so the after completes a pair. They get told.

Two-sided, with a real payoff at each step: the asker gets an answer, the answerer gets credit. Step 05 closes it by making the answerer the next notified person — which is what turns a loop into a habit rather than a one-off exchange.

04

Three post types, and the screens

In build order. Each one is shippable alone, and the first two need no new user behaviour at all — they are by-products of deciding and cooking.

Hero unit

The Save

Before and after. The kitchen you started with, the meal you made. Auto-drafted at Cook this, then asks for one photo when you are done.

The kitchen half needs no photo, so the post exists even if you never take one.

Interaction engine

The Rescue

"Five things, 30 minutes. Go." Others reply with a dish. The only post type where replying is more fun than scrolling — which is what generates comments instead of hoping for them.

KinniJije always answers, so a rescue is never left hanging.

Zero-effort supply

The Rundown

The existing daily rundown, posted to your own thread. Shareable outward. Makes the feed non-empty on day one with no behaviour change from anyone.

Private by default. Sharing is per-post and opt-in.

05

The privacy design, because this one can hurt people

I want to be direct about this rather than leave it as a launch checklist item. A feed of kitchen contents publishes how much food people have. In this market that is sensitive information, and a careless build would turn scarcity into a public fact about somebody who never agreed to share it.

Rules I would hold to

  • Opt in, per post. No account becomes public by existing. Default for every post is "just me".
  • A handle, never name. People typed their name for a receipt. A separate handle field, chosen deliberately, with name and email never leaving the server.
  • Never publish a whole kitchen. Only the ingredients a cook attached to a post. The full inventory stays private — it is the most revealing object we hold.
  • No scarcity leaderboards. No "emptiest kitchen", no streaks for cooking cheap. The joke must always be the cook's own to make.
  • Coarse location only. Area, never anything finer, and off by default.
  • Deletion is real. Removing a post removes the photo from the bucket, not just the row.

The framing does the work

The difference between cruel and fun is who is holding the pen. A rescue post is somebody asking for help on their own terms — that is dignified and it is the reason the format works. A system that surfaced the same kitchen unprompted, ranked against other people's, would be humiliating with identical data.

So: the cook writes the caption, the cook picks the ingredients shown, the cook chooses the audience. We never generate a line about somebody's kitchen being empty — the existing voice rule about never joking about the food covers this, and here it has teeth.

Also needed before launch, and genuinely unglamorous: report, block, and mute on every post and reply, plus a moderation queue. User-generated photos and free text mean this is not optional, and retrofitting it after the first incident is worse for everyone. Budget for it in stage two, not stage four.
06

What to A/B test

In priority order. Everything here assumes variant bucketing exists (§02) — without it these are before/after comparisons, not experiments. Note that test 0 is a guardrail, not a test: it is the one that can kill the feature.

Test 0 · guardrailPre-commit to this

Does the feed cannibalise cooking?

A feed can easily make people browse instead of cook. Primary metric: meals cooked per user per week (we already have CookedMealModel, so this needs no new instrumentation). If it drops, the feed has failed at the only job that matters, no matter how good engagement looks.

I would agree the kill threshold before launch, in writing. "Engagement up, cooking down" is the exact scenario where a team talks itself into shipping something that makes the product worse, because the engagement chart is the one on the slide.
Test 1 · the core hypothesis

Ingredients or plates?

Three arms, and this is the one worth spending traffic on:

  • A — plates only (the conventional build)
  • B — kitchens and befores only
  • C — both, paired before and after

Measure posts per user (supply) and replies per post (interaction). My prediction: B or C beats A on both, and A looks respectable on per-post quality while starving on volume.

If A wins, the thesis in §01 is wrong and we should drop this and build something else. That is a genuinely useful outcome for one experiment to produce.
Test 2 · the sticky mechanic

Generic hearts vs ingredient reactions

A heart on a post versus a reaction on a specific ingredient. Measure reactions per post — but the metric that actually matters is the second one:

does receiving a reaction predict the recipient posting again within 7 days? That is the real question behind "I want the idea to stick": does being complimented bring you back. If ingredient reactions win there, they are the mechanic to build everything else around.

Test 3 · highest leverage

The notification leg, on or off

"Someone suggested a dish for your kitchen" — on versus off. I would bet this single variable moves retention more than every visual decision in this document combined.

Measure D7 return rate and unsubscribe rate together. A notification that lifts returns while burning notification permission is not a win — it is borrowing from a channel we cannot get back.

Test 4 · run this first

Tab vs under the verdict

A dedicated Kitchens tab, versus a strip of recent saves appended under the decide verdict. The second is far cheaper, needs no new nav, and tests whether anybody wants this at all.

Despite being listed fourth, I would run this one first as a demand smoke test. If nobody taps a strip of saves under their own verdict, the tab will not save it, and we will have learned that for a few days of work instead of a quarter.

What I would not test

Copy, colours, button placement, card layout, icon choice. Those are decisions, not experiments — we have a design system that already answers them. Testing them early burns the traffic needed for the four questions above, and a win on button colour teaches us nothing about whether this feature should exist.

07

How I would sequence it

Deliberately back-loaded on cost. Stage 0 and 1 are small and answer the only question that matters — whether there is demand — before anything expensive gets built.

Stage 0 · ~1 week

Plumbing

Variant bucketing in lib/flags, variant stamped on every event, handle + avatar on the user behind an opt-in.

No user-visible feed yet. Reusable for every future experiment.

Stage 1 · ~1 week

Demand smoke test

Test 4's cheap arm only: a read-only strip of recent saves under the decide verdict. No posting, no replies.

Gate: does anyone tap it. If not, stop here — and that is a cheap, good outcome.

Stage 2 · ~3 weeks

Saves and rescues

Posting from the cook log, ingredient reactions, replies, the rundown in your own thread. Report/block/mute and the moderation queue ship here.

Runs tests 0, 1 and 2. Gate: cooked-per-week flat or up.

Stage 3 · 2–4 weeks

The loop closes

Batched activity digest by email under the existing frequency caps. Outward sharing needs an OG render path that does not exist — hence the range.

Runs test 3. Gate: D7 up without unsubscribes up.

Open questions for engineering

  • Which tab gets displaced? The bar is full at five. Saved and Market look weakest to me, but that is a product call I should not make alone — and it is the main reason to prove demand with the cheap arm first.
  • Outward sharing needs SSR or an OG render service. The SPA has one static index.html, so per-post OG tags cannot work today. Is the /hero route's capture approach extendable, or is this a new edge function? This decides whether stage 3 is two weeks or four.
  • A global timeline needs a new index. Every existing index is {ownerId, …}. Is a {visibility, createdAt} read model enough at our scale, or do we need fan-out from the start?
  • Can the job queue fan out a batched per-user digest, or is each sweep one-shot per user per day?
  • Do we extend cooked_meals with photo/note/rating/visibility, or add a separate cook_posts collection? I lean separate — the cook log is a private event spine and should stay one.
My recommendation: do stages 0 and 1 and nothing more until the strip earns it. The idea in §01 is genuinely good and genuinely differentiated — ingredients are an unoccupied space and the rescue format has real social energy — but "good idea" and "our users want this now" are different claims, and the second one costs two weeks to test instead of two months to assume. If the strip gets tapped, build the rest with confidence. If it does not, we have kept the roadmap.
