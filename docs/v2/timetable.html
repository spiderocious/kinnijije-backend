Product · The Plan · Proposal

The Plan

A multi-day meal timetable generated from the kitchen somebody already has — and the part no other meal planner does: the runway. Which ingredient runs out on which day, what spoils before you reach it, and what it costs to fix. Plus a morning reminder that carries tomorrow's shopping a day early, and a reusable number panel for every numeric input in the product.

01

This is two features, and they cost very different amounts

I went looking for the data before designing the screens, and the answer splits the brief cleanly in half. One half is a strong, differentiated feature we can build next sprint. The other half cannot be built honestly today at all. Worth separating now rather than discovering it mid-build.

Buildable now · the real idea

The runway

"Your rice runs out Thursday. The fish spoils before you reach Friday. Buy two things and the week holds." Everything this needs already exists:

  • StockItemModel holds quantity, unit, storage, addedAt
  • Recipe ingredients carry quantity and unit per line, plus serves on the meal
  • The catalogue has shelfLifeDays and costNgn for 417 items
  • A real unit system with families that refuse to cross-convert

That is a deterministic simulation: walk the plan day by day, subtract what each meal uses, flag the day each line hits zero or spoils. No AI needed, fully provable, and genuinely nobody else does it — every other planner assumes an empty kitchen and hands you a full shopping list.

Not buildable · needs data first

Calories and macros

Calorie targets, protein intake, weight loss/gain, muscle growth, fat avoidance. There is no nutrition data anywhere in the product — not on a meal, not on a catalogue ingredient, not in seed data. But it is worse than a gap:

The PRD already excludes this, on the record. From ui/insights/insights-more.tsx: "Not calories, not macros — the PRD explicitly excludes both… a product that cannot see what went into the pot has no business reporting a gram figure." There is a NutritionBalance component, and it deliberately shows only rough category shares — grains, protein, greens, other — never grams.

So building macro targets is not filling a gap, it is reversing a documented decision somebody made deliberately and wrote down. That needs to be an explicit call, not a side effect of a timetable sprint.

And the reasoning holds. For Nigerian food — where portion sizes are genuinely under-documented, and a congo of rice is an approximation by design — a confidently wrong 2,100 kcal is a number somebody will trust and act on. See §07.

My recommendation: build the runway first and ship it without macros. It is the differentiated half, it needs no new data, and it stands on its own as a reason to use the product weekly. Then add nutrition as a second, deliberate project with real data behind it — §07 sets out the cheapest honest path.
02

What the codebase gives us

The foundation here is unusually good — better than I expected before looking. Four things in particular are worth calling out because they shape the design.

Units that refuse to lie

shared/catalogue/units.ts defines unit families — weight, volume, count, bundle — and two units in different families never convert. "A bag" of rice has inBase: null because it has no defined weight.

The runway inherits this honesty: where it cannot convert, it says so rather than inventing a number. That is a feature, not a limitation.

Cost and shelf life, per item

Every catalogue item has costNgn and shelfLifeDays. That means the shopping list can carry a naira total, and the plan can route around spoilage — not just depletion.

The data file labels both as deliberate estimates "never presented as a price the cook will actually pay." The UI must honour that wording — see frame 06.

The UI is already built

@ui/planning already ships DayColumn, MealSlot, PlannedMeal, PortionScaler and PlanSummary — plus a WeekPlanScene demo registered as "Seven slots, designed empty-first."

Somebody designed this surface already and never wired it to a feature. DayColumn's own comment: "A day is a column, not a slot… a real week has more than one meal in a day."

This cuts stage 2 materially. The frames below should be reconciled against those components before any new ones are written — and DecideShell + use-swipe.ts give the day-by-day flow for free.

"Your week" is the other half

The existing week screen is retrospective — what you cooked, with an AI reading underneath. Its header states the house rule plainly:

"Every number here is computed in code. The AI reading sits underneath as an interpretation — never as the source of a figure."

The Plan is its forward-looking twin, and follows the identical rule. Natural pairing: Your week looks back, The Plan looks forward.

03

The number panel

A shared component, invoked from code, for every numeric input in the product — not just this feature. Full-screen keypad on mobile as a bottom sheet, centred modal on desktop. It exists because a tiny stepper and a native keyboard are both bad ways to enter "how many people am I cooking for" or "₦12,000".

The API

Imperative, promise-returning, so a caller awaits a value instead of managing modal state. It slots into the existing DrawerService, whose store already treats edges as sheets and the centre as the canonical modal — so the responsive behaviour is inherited, not reinvented. 

const n = await NumberPanel.ask({
  title: 'Cooking for',
  hint: 'How many plates per meal',
  value: 2,
  min: 1, max: 20,
  format: 'integer',
  unit: 'people',
})
// null when dismissed

// currency variant
await NumberPanel.ask({
  title: 'Shopping budget',
  value: 12000,
  min: 0, max: 200000,
  step: 500,
  format: 'currency',
  currency: 'NGN',
})

Rules

  • Blade on every key. A keypad is the most tempting place in the product to reach for rounded rectangles. It is not an exception.
  • Validation is live, never punitive. Out-of-range shows the limit and disables Done; it does not clear what was typed.
  • Currency formats as you type — grouping separators inserted, symbol fixed to the left, and tabular-nums so digits do not shift.
  • Done is always reachable by thumb on mobile — bottom of the sheet, full width.
  • Dismissing returns null, and the caller keeps its previous value. Cancelling is never destructive.
  • A real <input> underneath for hardware keyboards and screen readers; the keypad is an enhancement, not the only path.
Why not the OS keyboard? A numeric keyboard on Android still shows letters on many devices, resizes the viewport, and gives us no control over range or currency formatting. This is one of the few places a custom control genuinely beats the native one.

Weather, honestly

lib/weather gives summary, high, low and rain for one city, today — cached an hour, keyless Open-Meteo, and degrading to null rather than failing. The rundown already uses it.

So a 7-day plan cannot have real weather for day 6 without a forecast endpoint. Three rules follow, and they are in the frames above:

  • Weather nudges the order, never the dish. It swaps a soup and a grill between two days that both already fit the kitchen. It never adds a meal the kitchen cannot make.
  • Only shown where it is real. Today and the near days get a weather line; later days simply omit it rather than showing a guess.
  • The plan re-reads weather each morning in the reminder job, which is when it is both accurate and useful. A plan generated Monday should not claim to know Saturday.

If we want per-day weather across a full fortnight, that is a forecast upgrade to lib/weather — small, but it should be a deliberate ticket rather than assumed by this design.

05

More features around this

The brief asked for more ideas. These are the ones I would actually argue for — each is computable from data we already hold, and each makes the plan harder to abandon. Ranked by return against build cost, with the two I would cut at the bottom.

Build itCheap

1 · The plan heals itself

Plans break on contact with real life. You skip Tuesday, eat out Wednesday, burn the rice. A plan that cannot absorb that is abandoned by Thursday — and then never regenerated, because it has already proven unreliable.

So: "I didn't cook this" on every slot. The plan reflows, the runway recomputes, and the ingredients you did not use roll forward as surplus. Skipping should make the plan better informed, not broken.

I would treat this as part of v1, not an enhancement. It is the difference between a timetable and a wall chart.
Build itCheap

2 · Cook once, eat twice

A planner that ignores leftovers is lying about how people actually cook. A pot of stew is three meals. Model a cooked dish as portions produced against serves, and spend the remainder on later slots.

This is also the single biggest lever on the shopping total — it is how a week of 14 meals needs five cooking sessions instead of fourteen, which is both cheaper and closer to the truth.

Already half-expressed in the configure screen's "leftovers are fine" option.
Build itMedium

3 · Prep clustering

If Monday and Thursday both want blended pepper, say so: "Blend once on Sunday, it covers three meals." We already know every recipe's ingredient lines, so shared prep is a set intersection across the week.

This is the feature that makes the plan feel like it was written by somebody who has actually cooked — and it is a real time saving, not a UI flourish.

Build itMedium

4 · Market day as the anchor

Nobody shops daily. Ask which day is market day and plan around it: fresh things early in the cycle, hardy things at the end, and the shopping list delivered the evening before rather than whenever it happens to be generated.

Ties the plan to a rhythm that already exists in the user's week instead of asking them to adopt ours. It also gives the email digest an obvious, welcome send time.

Later

5 · Use-it-up mode

A plan whose only goal is to clear the fridge before anything spoils, ordered purely by expiry. The use_it_up notification preference already exists on the user — this is the surface that preference implies but never had.

Later

6 · Plan from a receipt

Receipt extraction already exists. Shop first, photograph the receipt, get a week planned from exactly what you bought. A strong moment — but it depends on extraction accuracy, so it follows rather than leads.

I'd cut this

Streaks and plan adherence scores

Tempting and wrong. A "you followed 4 of 7 days" score punishes people for having a life, and the failure mode is that they stop generating plans so they stop seeing the number. Measure adherence internally; never show it as a grade.

I'd cut this

Sharing plans between users

A plan is only meaningful against your kitchen — shared, it becomes a generic recipe list and loses the one thing that made it good. It also re-opens the kitchen privacy question from the shelved feed proposal for no real gain.

06

Where it lives — guests and members

You asked specifically about this. The two audiences want different things from the same engine, and the split follows a rule: a guest gets the demo, a member gets the habit.

Unregistered — on the marketing page

Worth being precise: / is the decide flow, not a landing page. Marketing lives at /why: SiteHero → KitchenConveyor → LiveMatch → TrustPlate → SitePricing → SiteFaq → FinalHob. So "put it on the landing page" means two different placements, and both are worth doing.

LiveMatch on /why is a self-playing demo that types ingredients and shows a match — the natural host.

I would add one section after LiveMatch — call it ThreeDays — that continues the same demo: the ingredients already on screen become a three-day plan, with days four to seven blurred behind the account wall with days 4–7 blurred. It follows the page's existing instinct: show it working, do not describe it.

Two placements, in priority order:

  • On /, after a guest's verdict — "Like that? See three days from the same kitchen." This is the higher-value one: they have already told us what they have, so the plan costs them nothing and the moment is warm.
  • On /why, a new section after LiveMatch — call it ThreeDays, continuing the same self-playing demo into a plan with days 4–7 blurred. Follows the page's existing instinct: show it working, do not describe it.

Mechanics, all of which already exist:

  • Three days free, held in sessionStorage on the versioned draft pattern — a mismatched version is discarded, never migrated
  • No runway for guests — it needs a persistent kitchen with quantities, so the gate is honest rather than arbitrary
  • optionalAuthenticate with the two-limiter switch decide already uses: anonymous keyed by IP, members by identity
  • carryOverDraft already does the replay onto a new account, best-effort, keeping the draft on failure so a hiccup never looks like a failed signup
Why not a dedicated /plan entry page? It would compete with decide for the same cold traffic and split the funnel. The plan is a second reason to stay, not a first reason to arrive — deciding one meal is far lower friction than planning a week.

Registered — the forward half of "Your week"

The existing week screen is retrospective. The Plan is its twin, and the cleanest framing for a member is one surface with two directions: what happened, and what is coming.

  • Entry from the existing "Your week" surface — a forward tab, not a sixth bottom tab. The mobile bar is full at five, and this does not earn a displacement on day one.
  • Entry from the decide verdict: "Make this the start of a week." The best moment to plan is just after a decision landed well.
  • Entry from the kitchen screen after a big stock-up, which is exactly when a week is worth planning.
  • The email digest carries today's slot through the existing rundown, so the plan has a pulse without push notifications existing.
One active plan at a time. Multiple overlapping plans make the runway meaningless — two plans cannot both spend the same rice. Regenerating replaces.
07

The honest path to nutrition

The brief put calories and macro targets at the centre. I have moved them to the end, and I want to be clear about why rather than quietly dropping them.

Why not now

There is no macro data on any meal or ingredient. To ship calorie targets this quarter we would have to ask a model to invent the numbers. A model will cheerfully return "Jollof Rice, 640 kcal, 18 g protein" for a dish whose real values depend on how much oil went in, whether it is a congo or a derica of rice, and how many people it is actually feeding.

Those numbers would look authoritative, be wrong by a wide margin, and — because the feature is framed around weight loss and muscle growth — be acted on. That is the one place in this product where a confident guess does real harm, and it is not a risk worth taking to hit a feature list.

What I would do instead, in order

Step one · now

Ship the signpost

The disabled "Health goals — coming later" line on the configure screen. It is instrumented, so we learn how many people tap it and whether this is actually wanted before funding the data work.

Costs nothing. Answers the demand question honestly.

Step two

Shape, not numbers

We can say truthful things without macros: "four of your seven dinners are swallow", "no fish or beans all week", "three fried meals in a row". That is derived from tags and ingredient groups we already have.

Honest, useful, and likely most of the real value people want from "nutrition" anyway.

Step three · the real fix

Nutrition per catalogue item

Per-100g macros on the catalogue items, not on recipes — then a meal's macros compute from its ingredient quantities and scale with servings automatically.

Sourced from a real food-composition table, operator-reviewed like recipe images already are. A bounded, one-time data project — and the only version worth shipping. It also needs gram-resolution of recipe units, which the no_fixed_size cases block.

This step reverses a written PRD decision. It needs a product owner to say so out loud, not a ticket.
Note on framing, whenever it does ship: "weight loss", "muscle growth" and "fat avoidance" are health claims. I would keep the product's language to what the food is — higher protein, lighter, more vegetables — and leave the medical framing alone. Same instinct as the existing voice rule about never joking inside an error: the product should not pretend to an authority it does not have.
08

How I would sequence it

The runway is the differentiator, so it goes first — and it is deterministic, which means it can be unit-tested properly rather than eyeballed.

Stage 1 · ~1.5 weeks

The simulator

Id-first stock matching, serving scaling, then the pure function: stock + ordered meals → per-ingredient runway with depletion and spoil dates. Heavy unit tests, including the unconvertible-unit case. The number panel ships here too — it is shared UI, not plan UI, and other screens want it.

Shippable alone: powers "you can cook 4 more meals before shopping" anywhere.

Stage 2 · ~1.5 weeks

Generate and preview

Deterministic plan builder over the existing matcher, then wire @ui/planning and DecideShell rather than building new components. One active plan per user.

Shorter than it looks because the plan UI already exists. Model writes per-slot prose only — never the structure.

Stage 3 · ~1 week

Runway + list

The runway screen, the two-tier shopping list with the naira total, "I didn't cook this" reflow, and the hand-off into the existing market list.

This is where the feature becomes differentiated rather than ordinary.

Stage 4 · ~1.5 weeks

Reminder + guest demo

The PLAN_REMINDER email kind, the "skip today" link, then the ThreeDays section, the blur wall, draft-survives-signup and anonymous rate limiting.

The reminder is what gives the plan a pulse; the guest demo is a conversion optimisation on something that must already be good.

The one real engineering risk

consume() matches stock by name, not catalogueId

stockService.consume exists, converts units and clamps at zero — but it resolves a recipe line to a stock row by name. That is tolerable for decrementing after one meal. For a seven-day forward simulation the errors compound: one unmatched spelling and the runway confidently tells somebody their rice is fine when it is not.

Both sides already carry catalogueId, and resolve(name) exists in the catalogue lookup. Hardening this to id-first matching with a name fallback is the first task of stage 1, not a follow-up — the whole feature's credibility rests on it.

Open questions

  • How complete are recipe quantities across all published meals? The schema allows quantity: null, and pantry staples are null by design. The seeds look well-populated, but the share of nulls on real non-staple lines is the number that decides how good the runway can be. Worth counting before stage 1 is estimated.
  • Serving scaling does not exist anywhere. serves is stored, PortionScaler is presentational only, and nothing multiplies ingredients by a target. The simulator needs this, and it is the second task of stage 1.
  • A new meal_plans collection, or extend decide_history? I lean new: a plan is mutable and ongoing, while decide history is an immutable log.
  • Can the rundown carry "today's slot" for users with an active plan, or does that need its own job?

Everything else is settled: the AI layer's withEnvelope zod pattern takes a MealPlanSchema directly, lib/jobs handles long generation with progress, and the 'deterministic' | 'ai_framed' provenance pattern is exactly what plan generation should copy.

My recommendation: build stages 1–3 and ship without any nutrition features at all. The runway is the genuinely novel thing here — every other meal planner starts from an empty kitchen, and ours is the only one that can tell you the day your rice runs out. That alone is worth a weekly visit, and roughly four weeks of work given how much already exists. Macros stay out until somebody decides, deliberately and on the record, to reverse the PRD exclusion and fund the data behind it.
